The mortgage market just had a rude awakening. After four straight weeks of falling rates, the 30-year fixed jumped from 7.11% to 7.28% in a single week, according to Freddie Mac's November 19 data. Mortgage News Daily's daily tracker was even grimmer, pegging the rate at 7.45% on that same date, nearly matching the 2026 peak of 7.40% hit on October 8. The culprit? October's CPI report came in at 6.5% year-over-year, the biggest inflation reading in four years, and that one number reshuffled everything.

Here's where it gets directly relevant to anyone buying, refinancing, or carrying an adjustable-rate mortgage: CME FedWatch data now shows 77 to 85% of interest-rate traders expect the Fed to raise its benchmark rate by another 25 basis points at the December 8 to 9 FOMC meeting. That would push the federal funds target range from 3.75 to 4.00% up to 4.00 to 4.25%. The Fed doesn't set [mortgage rates](/current-30-year-fixed-mortgage-rates-forecast/) directly, but when traders price in a hike with that level of confidence, mortgage rates tend to move before the meeting ever happens. They already did.

The window between now and December 9 is real, but it's narrow. If you're a buyer sitting on the fence, an ARM holder approaching a reset, or a HELOC borrower watching your payment creep up, understanding what's likely to happen, and what your actual options are, matters more than waiting to see how it plays out.

## Why This Rate Jump Is Different From the Others

Rates have spiked before in 2026 and then pulled back. The October 8 peak of 7.40% faded relatively quickly. So why treat this one differently?

Because the data driving it is stickier. A single month of hot inflation can be written off. A 6.5% annual CPI print, the highest in four years, signals that the Fed's rate campaign isn't finished yet, and bond markets are reflecting that. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield closely, and when inflation expectations rise, Treasury yields rise, and mortgage rates follow. That sequence is already in motion.

Mortgage application volume dropped 2.3% for the week ending November 13, after a brief 6.2% bounce the prior week, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association's weekly applications survey. That kind of volatility in applications, up sharply, then down again, reflects buyers who are hesitating and locking only to pull back. It's a fragile demand picture, and a December hike could break more of that confidence.

## What a December Hike Actually Does to Your Loan

The Fed rate and your mortgage rate are not the same thing, and conflating them is one of the most common mistakes buyers make. Here's a plain comparison of how different loan types respond to a Fed hike:

| Loan Type | How It Responds to a Fed Hike | Timing of Impact |
|---|---|---|
| 30-year fixed | Indirect. Already priced in by markets before the meeting. | Happening now, before Dec. 9 |
| 5/1 ARM (past initial period) | Rate adjusts based on index + margin at next reset date | At next adjustment, often 60–90 days |
| HELOC | Tied directly to prime rate, which moves with the Fed | Within 1–2 billing cycles |
| New ARM (initial period) | Rate locked during initial period, not affected yet | Only after fixed period ends |

If you have a HELOC with a $60,000 balance and your rate goes from 8.75% to 9.00% after a 25-bps hike, your monthly interest cost goes up by roughly $12.50. That sounds small until you realize the Fed has moved multiple times this cycle. Those small jumps stack.

Fixed-rate buyers face a different problem. The hike may be "baked in" to some degree, but lenders don't always price it in evenly, and volatility around the actual announcement can push rates in either direction for a short window.

## The Housing Market Context You Need to Know

Rates aren't rising into a hot market. Existing-home sales fell 5.5% in October to an annualized rate of 3.75 million, the lowest since 2010, per the National Association of Realtors. That's a housing market running on fumes.

What that means for buyers is counterintuitive. Yes, fewer people are buying. But sellers haven't dropped prices enough to compensate for 7%-plus rates in most markets. So affordability is still brutal, volume is down, and prices in many metros remain sticky. A December hike could push some marginal buyers out entirely and give the remaining inventory a bit more leverage in negotiations. That's not guaranteed. It depends heavily on your local market. But it's worth factoring in.

If you're a seller hoping rates fall enough to bring buyers back, a November 19 analysis described the current environment as a "tough backdrop" with little relief expected before year-end. The holiday slowdown, which typically softens activity from Thanksgiving through January anyway, is arriving just as rate pressure returns.

## What Buyers and ARM Holders Should Actually Do

This isn't a moment to panic. It is a moment to make a decision rather than drift.

**If you're under contract or close to making an offer:** Talk to your lender today, not next week, about a rate lock. Locks typically run 30 to 60 days and cost nothing on most standard purchase loans, though longer locks may carry a small fee. With the December 9 meeting less than three weeks out, locking before the FOMC decision removes one variable from an already complicated process.

**If you have an ARM approaching a reset:** Get the specifics from your servicer: what index your loan uses (usually SOFR now, sometimes still LIBOR-based older loans), what your cap structure is, and when your next adjustment date falls. A 25-bps hike won't necessarily trigger a catastrophic jump if your periodic cap limits increases to 2% per adjustment. But you should know the number before it shows up on your statement.

**If you have a HELOC:** This is the loan type most directly and immediately exposed to a Fed hike. If you've been thinking about converting the balance to a fixed-rate home equity loan, that conversation is worth having now. Rates on fixed home equity products have also risen, but locking eliminates the ongoing uncertainty.

**If you're still searching for a home:** A December hike doesn't necessarily mean you should rush into a purchase you're not ready for. Buying at 7.4% because you're scared of 7.65% rarely makes financial sense if the house isn't right or the numbers don't work. What it does mean is that if you're genuinely ready, waiting for a rate drop that may not come before Q1 2027 carries its own cost.

The situation is uncomfortable but not unusual. Rates have been elevated all year. The buyers who fared best in 2026 were the ones who stopped waiting for 2024's rates to return and made decisions based on current reality. That same logic applies now. Get a professional opinion from a licensed mortgage advisor or HUD-approved housing counselor who can run your specific numbers before you decide.
