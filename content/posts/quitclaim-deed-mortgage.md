A quitclaim deed can transfer ownership of a home without guaranteeing the title is clean. That's the whole deal, in one sentence. If there's a mortgage on the property, that transfer of ownership gets a lot more complicated, and most people don't realize just how complicated until they're already in trouble.

The confusion usually starts here: people think signing a quitclaim deed removes them from the mortgage. It doesn't. Ownership and debt are two separate things. You can sign away your legal interest in a property and still owe the bank every single dollar. That gap is where real financial damage happens.

## What a Quitclaim Deed Actually Does

A quitclaim deed transfers whatever ownership interest the grantor (the person signing it over) has in a property to the grantee (the person receiving it). No warranties, no [title search](/mortgage-title-search-process/) required, no guarantee that the title is free of liens or other claims. You're essentially saying "I hand over whatever I have, whatever that turns out to be."

This makes quitclaim deeds useful in a narrow set of situations: adding or removing a spouse after marriage or divorce, transferring property between family members, clearing up a name discrepancy in a title, or moving property into a trust. They're not designed for arm's-length sales between strangers, and any title company will tell you that.

What a quitclaim deed does not do: cancel a mortgage, release anyone from loan obligations, or notify the lender of anything. The deed is recorded at the county recorder's office. The lender doesn't sign it. The lender may not even know it happened, at least not immediately.

## The Due-on-Sale Clause Problem

Most mortgages include language called a due-on-sale clause, sometimes labeled an "acceleration clause." It says that if you transfer ownership of the property without the lender's permission, the lender can demand the entire loan balance immediately. The full remaining balance, due now.

A quitclaim deed counts as a transfer. So does adding someone to the title. So does moving the property into most trusts (though the Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 carves out certain exceptions, like transfers to a spouse or child where the borrower continues living there, or transfers into a revocable living trust where the borrower remains a beneficiary). Outside those specific exceptions, you're taking a real risk.

In practice, lenders don't always catch these transfers right away, and many don't immediately call the loan due when they do catch them. But "they might not notice" is a terrible financial strategy. If the lender does accelerate the loan and you can't pay it off or refinance immediately, you're looking at default.

The smart move before recording any quitclaim deed on a mortgaged property: call your lender, explain what you're trying to do, and ask in writing whether they'll waive the due-on-sale clause for this transfer. Some will, especially for transfers between family members or divorcing spouses. Get it in writing.

## Divorce Is Where This Bites Hardest

Divorce is probably the most common situation where people use quitclaim deeds on properties with mortgages, and it's also where the most damage gets done.

The scenario: one spouse is awarded the house in the divorce. The other spouse signs a quitclaim deed, transferring their ownership interest. Everyone thinks that's the end of it. The divorce decree says the spouse keeping the house is responsible for the mortgage. Done, right?

No. The divorce decree is an agreement between two spouses. The lender isn't a party to that agreement. The lender still has a contract with both original borrowers, and they can pursue either one if payments stop.

**Example:** Assume two borrowers co-signed a $320,000 mortgage. After divorce, one transfers ownership via quitclaim deed and moves out. The remaining owner misses three payments. The lender reports both original borrowers as delinquent. The departing owner, who no longer even has a legal ownership interest in the property, watches their credit score drop and potentially faces collection. If the remaining owner can't catch up, the lender can foreclose regardless of what the divorce decree says.

The only real solution: refinance the mortgage into the remaining owner's name alone. That takes the departing spouse off both the title and the loan. It requires the remaining spouse to qualify for the loan independently, which isn't always possible. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) has plain-language resources on what it means to be a co-borrower and how mortgage liability works, worth reading before you sign anything in a divorce situation.

## Assumptions and Quitclaims: What the Table Shows

There are times a quitclaim deed on a mortgaged property can work without triggering disaster. It depends heavily on the loan type and what the lender will allow. Here's how the common scenarios differ:

| Situation | Does Due-on-Sale Apply? | Lender Permission Needed? | What Usually Happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer to spouse (married) | Often waived by Garn-St. Germain | Notify lender; may not need approval | Usually low risk; confirm in writing |
| Transfer to child while parent lives there | Waived under Garn-St. Germain | Notify lender | Generally protected, but verify |
| Divorce: one spouse quits claim to other | Technically can trigger it | Lender may or may not agree to waive | Refinance is the safer path |
| Transfer to a sibling or other relative | Yes, applies | Yes, lender approval typically required | Lender may call loan due |
| Transfer to revocable living trust (borrower is beneficiary) | Waived under Garn-St. Germain | Notify lender; some have specific requirements | Usually permitted; check loan docs |
| Adding a new, unrelated person to title | Yes, applies | Yes | High risk; lender can accelerate loan |

The Garn-St. Germain exceptions are real protections, but they're not automatic shields. You still need to review your specific loan documents and, ideally, get written confirmation from your lender before proceeding.

## How to Actually Do This (Without Creating a Disaster)

If you've concluded that a quitclaim deed is the right tool and there's a mortgage involved, here's the order of operations that minimizes your risk:

1. **Read your mortgage documents first.** Look for "due-on-sale," "acceleration," or "transfer" language. This is usually in the first few pages of your deed of trust or mortgage document.

2. **Contact your lender before recording anything.** Explain the transfer. Ask whether it triggers the due-on-sale clause and whether they'll waive it. Ask for a written response. Keep records.

3. **Check whether a refinance makes more sense.** If the goal is to remove someone from the loan, a refinance does that cleanly. A quitclaim deed alone does not.

4. **Consult a [real estate](/financing-contingency-real-estate/) attorney in your state.** Quitclaim deeds are recorded at the county level and subject to state law. Forms vary by state. An attorney can also confirm whether the Garn-St. Germain exceptions apply to your specific situation.

5. **Record the deed at the county recorder's office.** Each county has its own process, filing fees, and requirements. Many have online portals now. Check your county's official website.

6. **Notify your [title insurance](/title-insurance-explained/) company** if you have an owner's policy. Some policies have clauses about transfers.

What can go wrong at step 2: the lender's customer service rep tells you verbally it's fine. That verbal okay means nothing. Push for written confirmation.

What can go wrong at step 5: the deed is prepared incorrectly (wrong legal description, wrong notarization) and gets rejected or, worse, recorded with errors that cloud the title later. [Freddie Mac's homebuyer education resources](https://myhome.freddiemac.com/) won't walk you through deed recording, but they're a good foundation for understanding how ownership and financing interact.

## Who Should Not Use a Quitclaim Deed

A quitclaim deed is the wrong tool if you're trying to protect yourself from a mortgage you co-signed. It offers no mortgage protection. It's also a bad idea if the property has title issues you're trying to quietly paper over, because a quitclaim deed transfers whatever you have, problems included.

If you're a buyer receiving a quitclaim deed, be cautious. You're accepting the property with no title warranty. If there are liens, judgments, or competing ownership claims you don't know about, they become your problem. At minimum, pay for a title search before accepting a quitclaim deed on any property with a mortgage.
