Closing day is the finish line, but it rarely feels like one. You'll spend most of it sitting at a table, signing papers you've never seen before, while a stranger tells you to "initial here" every 30 seconds. Knowing what's actually happening in that room changes everything. So here's the short version: a [mortgage closing](/mortgage-closing-costs-explained/) is the meeting where you legally transfer ownership and take on the loan. After that, the house is yours.

The longer version matters because mistakes made at closing are expensive and hard to undo.

## Who's In the Room

Depending on your state, closing happens at a title company office, an escrow office, or sometimes an attorney's office. Some states require a real estate attorney to be present. Others don't. It's worth knowing which situation applies to you before you show up.

The cast typically includes a closing agent (employed by the title company), sometimes your real estate agent, sometimes the seller's agent, and in purchase transactions, often the sellers themselves. Your lender probably won't be there in person. They've already done their part and transmitted the loan package to the title company. You may also have a notary if the closing agent isn't one.

Remote closings are increasingly common. Some lenders and title companies allow you to sign electronically through platforms like Notarize or via a Remote Online Notarization (RON). Rules vary by state, so confirm this option early if travel is a problem.

## What You're Actually Signing

This is where people tune out and just start initialing. Don't. You won't understand every page, and that's okay, but you should understand the ones that actually control your financial life.

**The Closing Disclosure (CD):** You should have received this at least three business days before closing. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) publishes a free, annotated sample of this form on its website, and reading it ahead of time is genuinely useful. The CD spells out your loan amount, interest rate, monthly payment, and all closing costs. At closing, you're confirming the numbers match what you agreed to. If they don't match your Loan Estimate, ask why before you sign.

**The Promissory Note:** This is your legal IOU to the lender. It says how much you borrowed, the interest rate, the repayment schedule, and what happens if you default. Read it.

**The Deed of Trust (or Mortgage, depending on your state):** This is what gives the lender the right to foreclose if you stop paying. It also gets recorded with the county, which is what creates the public record of the lien.

Everything else, the hazard insurance disclosures, the affidavits, the [servicing transfer](/mortgage-servicing-transfer/) notices, is supporting paperwork. Important, but not the main event.

Here's a quick look at the documents you'll see and what each one actually does:

| Document | What It Does | Can You Refuse to Sign? |
|---|---|---|
| Closing Disclosure | Confirms final loan terms and costs | No, but errors must be corrected before funding |
| Promissory Note | Your legal promise to repay the loan | No (without killing the transaction) |
| Deed of Trust / Mortgage | Secures lender's interest in the property | No (same) |
| Initial Escrow Statement | Shows projected escrow payments for year one | No |
| Servicing Disclosure | Tells you if the loan will be sold to another servicer | Acknowledgment only |
| Right of Rescission Notice | Gives you 3 days to cancel (refinances only) | Acknowledge receipt; cancellation is your choice |
| Title Insurance Commitments | Confirms title is insurable | Lender's policy is required; owner's policy is your call |

## The Money Part

You'll need to bring funds to closing. How much depends on your down payment and your closing costs combined, minus any credits you've negotiated with the seller or lender. The exact dollar amount is on the Closing Disclosure under "Cash to Close."

Most closings require a cashier's check or wire transfer. Personal checks are almost never accepted for large amounts. Wire fraud targeting homebuyers is a real problem: before you wire anything, call the title company directly using a phone number you found independently (not one from an email), and confirm the account details verbally. Scammers intercept email threads and swap in fraudulent wire instructions. It's not rare.

**Example:** Assume a $400,000 purchase price, a 10% down payment ($40,000), and closing costs of 3% ($12,000), with no seller credits. Your "Cash to Close" would be approximately $52,000. If you negotiated a $5,000 seller concession toward closing costs, you'd bring roughly $47,000. These percentages vary by loan type, lender, and location, so treat your actual Closing Disclosure as the only authoritative number.

Closing costs themselves typically break down into lender fees (origination, underwriting), third-party fees (appraisal, title search, title insurance), prepaid items (homeowners insurance premium, prepaid interest, initial escrow deposit), and government recording fees. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) publishes resources on typical closing costs by loan type if you want a benchmark for your area.

## The Walk-Through Nobody Talks About

The final walk-through happens before closing, usually within 24 hours. It's not a home inspection. Its only job is to confirm the property is in the same condition it was when you made your offer, that agreed-upon repairs were completed, and that nothing was removed that was supposed to stay.

A common mistake is treating this as a formality. It isn't. If you show up at closing and then mention that the refrigerator is gone or a ceiling fan was damaged, you've lost most of your leverage. If you notice a problem during the walk-through, you have options: delay closing, negotiate a price reduction, or put funds in escrow to cover the repair. Once you sign, your options narrow fast.

## After You Sign

Signing doesn't always mean you own the house that minute. There are still a few steps.

First, the title company sends the signed package back to the lender for review. This is called "funding." The lender confirms everything looks correct and wires the loan proceeds to the title company. Then the title company pays off any existing liens (like the seller's mortgage) and pays the seller.

After funding, the deed gets recorded with the county recorder's office. Recording is what legally transfers title to your name. In some states this happens the same day. In others, it takes a day or two.

You get the keys when the transaction is funded and recorded, or in some cases when the lender gives the title company explicit authorization to release them. Your agent or closing agent will tell you exactly when that moment is.

For a refinance, there's a mandatory 3-business-day "right of rescission" window after signing. You can cancel without penalty during that period. Only after it expires does the lender release funds. This window does not exist for purchase transactions.
