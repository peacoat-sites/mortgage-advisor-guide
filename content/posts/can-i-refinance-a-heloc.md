Yes, you can refinance a HELOC. How you do it, and whether you should, depends on where your HELOC is in its life cycle and what problem you're actually trying to solve.

A home equity line of credit works in two phases: a [draw period](/heloc-draw-period-explained/) (usually ten years) where you can borrow against the line and make interest-only payments, followed by a repayment period (typically ten to twenty years) where the balance amortizes and your payment jumps. That payment shock at the transition point is one of the most common reasons people go looking for a refinance. The other big driver is rate. HELOCs are almost always variable-rate products tied to the prime rate, and when rates run high, that floating rate can sting.

You've got three main paths: refinance the HELOC into a new HELOC, convert it into a fixed-rate [home equity loan](/home-equity-loan-rates/), or roll the HELOC balance into a new first mortgage through a cash-out refinance. Each path has a different cost, a different risk profile, and works better in different situations.

## Why the Draw Period Ending Changes Everything

During the draw period, most HELOCs charge interest only on what you've borrowed. The math is simple: if you've drawn $60,000 at 9% interest, your monthly payment is $450. When the repayment period kicks in and the same $60,000 amortizes over fifteen years at the same rate, you're looking at roughly $608 a month. That's a 35% jump without rates moving an inch. If rates have also risen since you opened the line, the payment increase is worse.

This is why timing matters. Refinancing before your draw period closes gives you more options and more leverage with lenders. Once you're in repayment, you've essentially got a term loan already, and your negotiating position shifts.

A lot of people don't realize the draw period end date is in their original HELOC agreement. Find it now. If you have less than two years left on the draw period and a balance you can't comfortably absorb into an amortizing payment, start the refinance conversation today, not six months from now.

## The Three Paths, Compared

Here's a plain comparison of your choices, using a $60,000 HELOC balance as the working assumption throughout:

| Option | Rate Type | Closing Costs (typical) | Resets First Mortgage? | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New HELOC | Variable (prime + margin) | $0-$500 (some lenders waive) | No | Borrowers who want continued flexibility and expect rates to fall |
| Fixed Home Equity Loan | Fixed | $500-$3,000 | No | Borrowers who want predictable payments without touching the first mortgage |
| Cash-Out Refinance (first mortgage) | Fixed or ARM | 2%-5% of total loan amount | Yes | Borrowers with a small first mortgage balance or who want to consolidate everything |

The cash-out refi is the nuclear option. It [makes sense](/when-does-it-make-sense-to-refinance-your-mortgage-in-2026/) when your first mortgage balance is small relative to your home value, when you want to consolidate at one fixed rate, or when mortgage rates have dropped meaningfully below your current first mortgage rate. If your first mortgage is at 3.25% from a few years back, a cash-out refi today means giving up that rate on the whole balance. That's a significant cost most people underestimate.

The fixed home equity loan is frequently the most practical middle path. You lock in a rate, get predictable payments, and leave your first mortgage untouched.

## What Lenders Actually Check

Underwriting a HELOC refinance looks a lot like underwriting the original loan, with a few wrinkles.

**Equity first.** Most lenders want your combined loan-to-value (CLTV), meaning your first mortgage balance plus the full HELOC credit limit (not just what you've drawn), to stay at or below 85% of the home's current appraised value. Some go to 90%, but those products carry higher rates.

**Example:** Assume your home appraises at $400,000, you owe $250,000 on the first mortgage, and your HELOC limit is $80,000 (with $60,000 drawn). The CLTV calculation uses the full limit: ($250,000 + $80,000) / $400,000 = 82.5%. That gets you in the door at most lenders. If the HELOC limit were $110,000, you'd be at 90%, which is tighter.

Credit scores matter, as they always do. Most lenders want at least a 680 for home equity products, with better pricing above 720 and 740. Debt-to-income (DTI) ratios are checked too. If you want to see where your DTI lands before you apply, the [Debt Income Calc](/debt-to-income-calculator/) will walk you through the numbers lenders will run. Generally, lenders want your total housing costs plus all other monthly debt obligations to stay below 43% of gross monthly income, though some go higher with compensating factors.

Income documentation is fully required. You're not getting a no-doc HELOC refinance. W-2s, tax returns, and pay stubs are standard. Self-employed borrowers typically need two years of business and personal returns.

One thing that trips people up: if you've recently used the HELOC for something a lender considers risky, like funding a business or buying investment property, some lenders will probe that in underwriting. It won't necessarily disqualify you, but be prepared to explain the purpose.

## The Real Costs Nobody Leads With

A new HELOC from your existing lender is often the cheapest option by a wide margin. Many banks will modify or extend the draw period for established customers with little or no cost, though this is a conversation you have to initiate directly.

Switching to a new lender for a fresh HELOC is where fees sneak in. Check your existing HELOC agreement for a prepayment penalty or early-closure fee. These typically run $300 to $500 and kick in if you close the line within two or three years of opening it. That's usually worth paying if the new rate or terms are significantly better, but factor it in.

Fixed home equity loans carry standard closing costs, often $500 to $3,000, depending on the loan size and lender. Some institutions offer "no-closing-cost" options where they roll the costs into the rate, which costs more over time but lowers the upfront burden.

Cash-out refinances are expensive. On a $300,000 new first mortgage, 2%-5% in closing costs means $6,000 to $15,000. That's real money, and it matters for break-even analysis. If you're going the cash-out route, run the numbers on how long until the payment savings (if any) offset the closing costs. The [Refinance Break Even](/refinance-break-even-calculator/) tool makes this calculation straightforward.

## Tax Angle (Don't Skip This)

Interest on a HELOC is deductible only if the funds were used to "buy, build, or substantially improve" the home securing the debt. That's been the rule since 2018 and it hasn't changed. If you used your HELOC for a kitchen addition, the interest is likely deductible (subject to the $750,000 combined mortgage debt limit for itemizers). If you used it to pay off credit cards or fund a vacation, it's not.

When you refinance, the same use-of-funds test follows the debt. Refinancing a HELOC you used to pay off a car doesn't suddenly make the interest deductible. The IRS looks at the original purpose.

One 2026 development that's genuinely useful for some people: mortgage insurance premiums are deductible again for itemizers starting this year, with income limits applying. This doesn't directly affect HELOC refinancing decisions for most people, but it's worth knowing if you're weighing a cash-out refi into a high-LTV first mortgage that requires PMI. The CFPB's [owning a home resources](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) have solid, plain-language guidance on mortgage-related tax rules and when to loop in a tax professional, which you should do for anything complex.

## Who Should Not Refinance Their HELOC Right Now

If you've drawn almost nothing on the HELOC, closing it and opening a new one is a lot of paperwork for minimal benefit. Consider whether your problem is structural (variable rate, upcoming payment shock) or just theoretical discomfort with the rate number on your statement.

If your home's value has dropped and your CLTV is above 85%, you may not qualify for a refinance at all without paying down the balance first. The same goes for a credit score that's taken recent hits. Getting declined and generating a hard inquiry hurts your position for the next application.

And if you're within two to three years of paying the HELOC off entirely, the closing costs of any refinance option probably outweigh the benefit unless the rate difference is dramatic.
