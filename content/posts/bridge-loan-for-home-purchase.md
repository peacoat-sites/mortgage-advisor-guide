A bridge loan lets you buy your next home before your current one sells. That's the whole idea. It's a short-term loan secured against the equity in your existing home, designed to "bridge" the gap between the two transactions. If you've found the house you want but haven't sold yet, a bridge loan can keep you from losing it.

The catch is that bridge loans are expensive, structurally awkward, and genuinely risky if your home doesn't sell quickly. They're not a universal solution. For some buyers they're the right tool; for others they're a way to end up carrying two mortgages plus a bridge loan simultaneously while watching their savings drain.

## How the Money Actually Flows

A bridge loan is usually structured one of two ways. In the first, the lender loans you a lump sum based on a percentage of your current home's appraised value (typically 70-80% of the home's value minus whatever you still owe on your existing mortgage). You use that cash as a [down payment](/down-payment-from-401k/) on the new home, while your existing mortgage stays in place. In the second, the bridge loan folds your existing mortgage balance into it, so you have a single loan covering both properties until you close on the sale.

Lenders typically won't let you borrow more than 80% of the combined value of both properties. Some lenders cap the bridge amount at 80% of your current home's value minus the remaining balance.

**Example:** Suppose your current home is appraised at $400,000 and you owe $150,000 on the mortgage. At 80% loan-to-value, the lender will lend against up to $320,000. After subtracting your $150,000 balance, that leaves roughly $170,000 available as a bridge loan. If your new home costs $450,000 and you want to put 20% down, you need $90,000. That math works. But if you owed $280,000 on the existing home, you'd only have $40,000 available, which almost certainly doesn't work for a 20% down payment on the same purchase.

The lender will also look at your debt-to-income ratio counting all three payments: your existing mortgage, your new mortgage, and the bridge loan. That's the number that kills a lot of bridge loan applications. Use our [debt-to-income calculator](/debt-to-income-calculator/) to see where you'd land before you approach a lender.

## What It Actually Costs

Bridge loans are not cheap. That's not a complaint, just a fact worth pricing out before you commit.

| Cost Component | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | Prime + 1.5% to 3% | Variable or fixed; often higher than a HELOC |
| Origination fee | 1% to 3% of loan amount | Paid at closing or rolled into loan |
| Appraisal fee | $400 to $700 | Required on current home |
| Title/escrow fees | $500 to $1,500 | Varies by state |
| Loan term | 6 to 12 months (sometimes 24) | Extensions usually cost another fee |
| Prepayment penalty | Sometimes 0, sometimes 1-2% | Read the note carefully |

**Example:** Using the $170,000 bridge loan from above, at a 9% interest rate (not unreasonable when standard 30-year rates are around 6.5-7%), you'd pay roughly $1,275 per month in interest alone. If your home takes five months to sell, that's about $6,375 in interest, plus possibly $3,400 to $5,100 in origination fees. Total cost: $10,000 or more, before any extension fees. For many buyers, that's worth paying to secure a home in a competitive market. But it's real money, and it assumes the home sells on schedule.

## Who Bridge Loans Work For (and Who They Don't)

Bridge loans make the most sense in a few specific situations. You're in a hot market where sellers won't accept contingency offers. Your current home has significant equity. You have strong credit and income to carry multiple payments for a few months. And you have confidence, based on actual comparable sales data, that your home will sell quickly.

They're genuinely risky for buyers who: have thin equity in their existing home, are in a slow or softening market, have income that's already stretched covering one mortgage, or whose current home has deferred maintenance that could suppress the sale price or delay closing.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) is blunt about short-term lending costs: fees and rates that look manageable month-to-month can compound into serious problems if the original timeline stretches. A bridge loan that was supposed to last four months becomes a ten-month problem if the market softens, and "I didn't expect this" is not a defense when you're in default.

One group that often overlooks a better option: buyers who have enough equity to open a HELOC on their current home *before* listing it. HELOCs have much lower rates and fees than bridge loans. The catch is that most lenders won't approve a new HELOC once you've listed your home for sale, so you'd need to open it while you're still living there with no active listing. If you have time to plan, a HELOC can serve the same bridging function at a fraction of the cost.

## The Application Process, Step by Step

The timeline is tight by design, so knowing what you're walking into helps.

1. **Get pre-qualified for the bridge loan** before you make an offer on the new home. Lenders will want an appraisal of your current home, your existing mortgage statement, two years of tax returns, proof of income, and a credit check. Expect 7-15 business days for underwriting.

2. **Get the new home under contract.** Your offer will likely be non-contingent on the sale of your current home, which is partly why you're doing this.

3. **List your current home.** Lenders will want to see it listed or will want confirmation it's going to market quickly. Some require the listing before funding.

4. **Close on the new home** using the bridge funds as your down payment. At this point you're carrying the bridge loan plus your existing mortgage plus your new mortgage. That's three loans. This is where people start to panic if the timeline slips.

5. **Sell your current home and [pay off](/how-to-set-up-biweekly-mortgage-payments-to-pay-off-faster/) the bridge loan.** At closing of the sale, your settlement statement will include a payoff of both your original mortgage and the bridge loan.

What can go wrong at each stage: appraisal comes in low (reducing your available bridge funds), your current home sits on the market, the buyer of your current home has financing fall through, or interest rates move enough to affect your new loan's approval. The [Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA)](https://www.fhfa.gov/) publishes data on conforming loan limits and market conditions that can help you gauge whether your current home's value assumptions are realistic.

## Alternatives Worth Pricing First

Before committing to a bridge loan, run the numbers on these:

A **HELOC** on your current home (if opened before listing) typically carries rates much closer to the prime rate and has minimal [origination fees](/how-much-are-mortgage-origination-fees-on-a-300k-loan/). The limitation is timing: you need to plan several months ahead.

A **contingency offer** (making your purchase contingent on the sale of your current home) is free. Sellers hate them, especially in competitive markets. But if you're in a slower market or working with a motivated seller, it's worth trying before spending $10,000+ on a bridge loan.

A **piggyback loan** or adjusting your down payment could let you buy the new home with what cash you have, then pay down or recast the mortgage after the sale. It depends on your savings and what down payment percentage the new lender requires.

Renting your current home for a period is worth modeling too, particularly if you're not in a rush. A lot of "I need a bridge loan" situations turn into "actually, I can close on the new home with savings and bridge the gap myself" after a detailed budget review.
