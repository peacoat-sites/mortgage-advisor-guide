Most buyers don't realize they can take over someone else's mortgage, at the original interest rate, and potentially save hundreds of dollars a month compared to getting a new loan today. That's what an assumable mortgage is. The seller transfers their existing loan to you, you pay the difference between the sale price and the remaining balance in cash (or with a second loan), and you're locked into whatever rate they got years ago.

This matters a lot [right now](/should-you-take-an-arm-right-now-to-beat-high-fixed-rates/). Rates climbed sharply from historic lows, and millions of homeowners are sitting on loans from earlier in the decade with rates well below what a new borrower can get today. If you can find one of those loans and qualify to assume it, you're inheriting a genuinely valuable asset.

## Which Loans Are Actually Assumable

Conventional loans almost never are. Most [Fannie Mae](/fannie-mae-ends-limited-review-what-condo-buyers-must-know-now/) and Freddie Mac loans contain a "due-on-sale" clause that requires the balance to be paid in full when the property transfers. So if the seller has a standard 30-year conventional mortgage, it's not on the table.

[FHA loans](/why-fha-loans-now-dominate-new-home-sales-applications/) are assumable by any creditworthy buyer, regardless of whether they've ever used an FHA loan before. USDA loans are assumable with lender and USDA approval. VA loans are assumable, but with a catch that matters a lot to veteran sellers (more on that below).

The loan type is stamped on the original closing documents and shows up in the listing if the agent knows to flag it. You can also check the Closing Disclosure or ask the seller directly. Some real estate listing platforms have started tagging assumable loans as a feature, so it's worth filtering for that if the tool you're using offers it.

## The Math: Why This Can Be a Big Deal

**Example:** Assume a home is priced at $450,000. The seller has an FHA loan with a remaining balance of $310,000, originated several years ago at 3.25%. Current 30-year rates are around 7%. You'd take over the $310,000 loan at 3.25% and need to cover the $140,000 gap separately.

Monthly payment on $310,000 at 3.25% (30-year): roughly $1,349.

Monthly payment on a new $310,000 loan at 7.00%: roughly $2,062.

That's a difference of about $713 a month on just the assumed portion. Over five years, that's nearly $43,000. The assumed rate is doing real work here.

The harder problem is that $140,000 gap. You'd need either cash, a second mortgage, or a combination. Second mortgages on assumed loan deals are specialized; not every lender does them, and the rates aren't going to be 3.25%. You need to run the blended cost of both loans against what you'd pay on a single new mortgage to know whether it still makes sense. Use the [mortgage payment calculator](/mortgage-payment-calculator/) to model both scenarios before you commit.

## The Qualification Process (and What Can Slow It Down)

Assuming a loan is not a shortcut past underwriting. You still need to qualify with the lender, provide full income documentation, and meet the same credit standards as a new borrower. With FHA loans, that typically means a minimum 580 credit score for 3.5% down, though lenders can impose higher standards.

The process runs through the existing loan servicer, not just any lender, and servicers vary wildly in how well they handle assumptions. Some have dedicated assumption departments. Others treat it like a foreign concept and route it through general customer service, adding weeks or months to the timeline. Realistically, you should budget 60 to 90 days for the process to close, and some transactions take longer.

What can go wrong at each step:

1. **Servicer identification and contact.** The servicer may have changed since the loan originated. Check the monthly mortgage statement or the MERS servicer lookup tool at mersinc.org.
2. **Assumption packet submission.** Missing documents stall everything. Get the full checklist upfront.
3. **Underwriting.** Same risk as a new loan: if your DTI is too high or credit score too low, you don't get the assumption. Check where you stand with the [debt-to-income calculator](/debt-to-income-calculator/) before you apply.
4. **Title and closing.** The seller's name comes off the loan. This needs to be coordinated precisely with the title company.

Sellers should know that until the assumption is complete and their name is off the loan, that debt still shows on their credit. Delays hurt them, too.

## The VA Loan Wrinkle

A VA loan can be assumed by anyone who qualifies, veteran or not. That sounds great, and the rate savings can be substantial. But here's what stops a lot of sellers cold: when a non-veteran assumes a VA loan, the seller's VA entitlement stays tied up in that loan until it's paid off. The seller can't use their full VA benefit to buy another home in the meantime, unless they go through a process to restore it, which requires the assuming borrower to be a veteran and to substitute their own entitlement.

For a veteran seller who wants to buy another home with VA financing, this is a real problem. It doesn't kill every VA assumption deal, but it has to be discussed explicitly and early. If you're a veteran buyer assuming a VA loan from another veteran, substituting entitlement cleans this up for everyone.

Freddie Mac's home buyer resources cover VA entitlement basics if you want to understand the mechanics before talking to a lender, and a [HUD-approved housing counselor](https://www.hud.gov/i_want_to/talk_to_a_housing_counselor) can walk through the broader implications at no cost.

## Who Should Skip This

Not every buyer who finds an assumable loan should chase it. If the gap between the remaining balance and the purchase price is very large and you don't have the cash or access to a reasonably priced second mortgage, the blended rate might not beat a standard new loan. Run the actual numbers before you decide.

Also, if you need to close in 30 days, an assumption probably won't get there. The timeline alone can disqualify you in a competitive market where sellers want speed.

If you're buying a newer construction home or one with a conventional loan, this whole conversation is moot. Assumable loans are only on FHA, VA, or USDA properties where the seller has held the loan through a rate environment that's meaningfully better than today's.

### Frequently Asked Questions

#### Can I assume a mortgage without telling the lender?

No. Attempting to transfer a loan without lender approval is a violation of the loan terms and can trigger the due-on-sale clause, making the full balance immediately due. Every assumption goes through the servicer with full documentation.

#### Do I need a down payment to assume a mortgage?

You need enough cash (or a second loan) to cover the difference between the purchase price and the remaining loan balance. There's no fixed "down payment" amount for assumptions, but that gap can be substantial on appreciated properties.

#### How long does a mortgage assumption take to close?

Plan for 60 to 90 days as a reasonable baseline. Some servicers move faster; others are significantly slower. Build that timeline into your purchase agreement and ask the servicer directly about their current processing times before you sign a contract.

#### Is there a fee to assume a mortgage?

Yes. FHA allows servicers to charge an assumption processing fee, and VA loans have their own allowable fees. The amounts vary by servicer, but you should ask for the full fee schedule upfront. It's typically much smaller than origination fees on a new loan.

#### What happens if the seller has already paid down a lot of the loan?

A lower remaining balance means a bigger gap to cover, which makes the deal harder to structure. The rate savings on the assumed portion are still real, but you need more cash or financing to bridge the difference. That's the scenario where the math sometimes doesn't work out in the buyer's favor.
