The period between signing your purchase contract and sitting at the closing table is exactly when buyers make the mistakes that blow up deals. Not after closing. Not before the offer. Right in that window, usually 30 to 60 days, when you think the hard part is over.

It isn't. Your lender is still watching you. They'll pull your credit again before funding, verify your employment, and check your bank statements. Anything that changes your financial picture can stall your closing, raise your rate, or kill the loan entirely. Here's what to avoid, and why each one actually matters.

## Don't Touch Your Credit

This one trips up buyers constantly, because it feels harmless. You're about to own a home, so you think ahead and open a store card for appliances or finance a new couch. The furniture store sells you on 0% for 18 months. What's the problem?

The problem is that new credit account just showed up on your report, your available debt went up, and your scores may have dropped from the hard inquiry. Your lender sees all of it. If your debt-to-[income ratio](/debt-to-income-ratio-for-mortgage/) (the percentage of your gross monthly income going to debt payments) creeps past their limit, you can lose your approval or get bumped to a worse loan program.

Don't apply for new credit of any kind. No store cards, no car loans, no "pre-approval" for anything. If a creditor asks to pull your credit during this period, say no and explain you're closing on a house.

Also: don't close old accounts. It sounds counterintuitive, but closing a card reduces your available credit and can ding your score by increasing your overall utilization. Leave everything exactly as it was when you got approved.

## Don't Change Jobs (Even for a Better One)

A job change feels like good news. More money, right? Your lender disagrees, at least for now.

[Mortgage approval](/mortgage-approval-timeline/) is tied to documented, stable income. If you switch employers, your lender usually needs 30 days of pay stubs from the new job before they can count that income. If you're salary-to-salary in the same field, it's sometimes workable with a letter from the new employer. But if you're moving from salaried to self-employed or commission-based, you're looking at a potentially much longer delay, because lenders typically want two years of self-employment history to use that income in underwriting.

This isn't a situation where you can close fast and sort it out later. The lender will verify your employment status within days of closing, often by calling your HR department directly.

If a job change is unavoidable, tell your loan officer immediately. They may be able to work around it. Hiding it is never the right move; they'll find out anyway.

## The Bank Account Rules Nobody Explains

Your closing disclosure will show your down payment, closing costs, and cash-to-close figure. Your lender needs to verify that money exists, is yours, and didn't just appear out of nowhere. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's mortgage resources at [consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) walk through what lenders are required to document, which is worth reading before your loan goes to underwriting.

Here's where buyers get surprised:

**Large deposits.** If an unusually large sum drops into your account, underwriting will flag it. They'll ask for a "letter of explanation" and documentation of where it came from. This applies even if it's a gift from family. Gift funds are allowed for many loan types, but they require a signed gift letter stating the money doesn't need to be repaid, plus documentation of the transfer. If you know a gift is coming, ask your loan officer how to handle it before the money moves.

**Moving money around.** Shuffling funds between accounts creates a paper trail that underwriters have to follow. Every transfer needs to be explainable. If you're consolidating savings into one account for closing, your loan officer should know.

**Cash deposits.** Bank tellers hand you a deposit receipt. Mortgage underwriters want to know where the cash came from. Cash deposits without a verifiable source (like selling a car, in which case keep the bill of sale) are a serious problem. Anti-money-laundering compliance is real, and lenders have no flexibility there.

## What Happens If You Do Any of This

A concrete comparison helps here, because "your loan could be affected" is too vague.

| Action | Likely consequence | Timeline impact |
|---|---|---|
| Open a new credit card | Credit inquiry drops score; new minimum payment raises DTI | May require re-underwriting; delays 1-3 weeks |
| Finance a car or furniture | New monthly payment raises DTI; possible denial | Loan may be restructured or denied |
| Quit or change jobs | Income unverifiable until new pay stubs exist | 30+ day delay minimum; self-employment = 2 years |
| Make large unverified deposit | Underwriting freeze pending documentation | 3-10 business days per missing document |
| Move large sums between accounts | Paper trail must be traced and documented | 3-10 business days per missing document |
| Close a credit card | Available credit drops; score may fall | Re-underwriting if score crosses lender threshold |
| Pay off a collection account | Score can sometimes drop short-term (surprising but true) | Discuss with loan officer before doing this |

That last row deserves a word. Paying off an old collection sounds responsible, but it can reactivate a dormant account on your credit report and temporarily drop your score. Don't make any moves on old collections during this window without talking to your loan officer first.

## Don't Make Big Purchases, Full Stop

No car. No boat. No major appliances on a new credit account. No renovations you're financing. Even paying cash for a large purchase can deplete the funds your lender has verified for closing. If your verified checking account drops significantly between approval and closing, you may need to explain where the money went or document new reserves.

**Example:** Assume you have $40,000 verified in your account and your cash-to-close is $38,000. You buy a riding mower for $3,500 cash two weeks before closing. Now your account shows $36,500. You're below your required cash-to-close figure. Even if you have other accounts, those now need to be sourced and verified, which takes time you may not have.

The rule is simple: if you weren't going to do it the week before you got pre-approved, don't do it now.

## Don't Go MIA on Your Lender

Underwriting moves fast when everyone responds quickly and grinds to a halt when they don't. If your loan officer emails asking for a pay stub, a utility bill for verification, or an explanation letter, respond that day. Missing a 48-hour window can push your closing date, and a pushed closing date can cost you money if you're locked into a rate.

Rate locks have expiration dates. The Federal Housing Finance Agency publishes information on conforming loan standards, including how locks work in the conventional loan market, at [fhfa.gov](https://www.fhfa.gov/). Most locks run 30, 45, or 60 days. Extending a lock costs money, typically a fraction of a percent of the loan amount, which on a $400,000 loan is not a trivial number.
