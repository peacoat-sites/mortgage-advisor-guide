Buying a second home sounds straightforward until your lender starts asking questions you didn't expect. The [mortgage rules](/respa-mortgage-rules/) for a second home are genuinely different from your primary residence, and those differences affect your down payment, your rate, and whether you can even qualify. The short version: lenders treat second homes as higher risk, so they ask for more from you upfront and charge a little more in interest.

Understanding why helps you make smarter decisions before you apply.

## What Lenders Actually Mean by "Second Home"

This is where people get tripped up. A second home, in mortgage terms, isn't just any property you own besides your primary residence. It has to meet a specific set of conditions or the lender reclassifies it as an [investment property](/investment-property-mortgage/), which carries stricter rules and higher rates.

To qualify as a second home, the property generally must be:

- Occupied by you for at least some portion of the year (not just sitting as a pure rental)
- Located a reasonable distance from your primary home (lenders are skeptical of a "vacation home" that's 12 minutes away)
- A single-unit property (not a duplex or triplex)
- Not managed by a rental management company or listed as a full-time rental

That last one matters more than people realize. Listing a property on a vacation rental platform for 48 weeks a year and calling it your second home doesn't hold up. If you're renting it out more than you're using it, most lenders will treat it as an investment property regardless of what you call it. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) has plain-language resources on how lenders classify properties, and it's worth reading before you apply.

## Down Payment and Rate: The Real Numbers

Second home loans require more skin in the game than primary residence loans. Here's how the requirements typically compare across common loan types:

| Loan Type | Primary Residence Min. Down | Second Home Min. Down | Investment Property Min. Down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional (Fannie/Freddie) | 3%–5% | 10% | 15%–25% |
| FHA | 3.5% | Not eligible | Not eligible |
| VA | 0% (for eligible veterans) | Possible with entitlement remaining | Not eligible |
| Jumbo | Varies by lender (often 10%–20%) | Often 20%–30% | Often 25%–35% |

FHA and USDA loans are strictly for primary residences. If you're buying a beach house or mountain cabin, you're looking at conventional financing or a jumbo loan if the price clears the conforming loan limits set by the [Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA)](https://www.fhfa.gov/). The FHFA updates those limits annually, so check their site for current figures rather than relying on a number you read somewhere last year.

On the rate side, expect to pay somewhere between 0.25% and 0.75% more than you'd pay on a comparable primary residence loan. That range isn't fixed. It depends on your [credit score](/credit-score-for-mortgage-approval/), your loan-to-value ratio, and market conditions. Even a quarter-point premium adds meaningful cost over a 30-year loan.

**Example:** Assume a $450,000 second home purchase with 20% down ($90,000), a $360,000 loan. At a primary residence rate of 6.75%, the monthly principal and interest payment is roughly $2,335. At a second home rate of 7.25% (a 0.50% premium), that payment rises to about $2,457. That's roughly $122 per month more, or just over $43,900 across a 30-year term. These figures use assumed rates for illustration; your actual rate will vary.

## Qualifying With Two Mortgages

You'll carry two mortgage payments simultaneously, and lenders count both against your debt-to-income ratio. That's the ratio of your gross monthly income to your monthly debt obligations, and conventional lenders generally want to see it at 45% or below (some allow up to 50% with strong compensating factors like high reserves or excellent credit).

The income you can use to offset the second home's payment is your actual documented income. Anticipated rental income usually doesn't count unless you've got a track record and tax returns showing it. This catches a lot of buyers off guard. They plan to rent the property most of the year and use that rent to cover the mortgage, but the lender won't give them credit for income that hasn't happened yet.

Reserves matter here too. Many lenders require two to twelve months of mortgage payments held in liquid assets for each property you own. So if you're carrying a primary mortgage of $2,000 a month and a new second home payment of $2,400, six months of reserves means keeping about $26,400 in savings that you don't touch at closing. Requirements vary by lender, so ask directly.

## The Rental Rule You Can't Ignore

There's a specific IRS rule that determines how your second home is taxed, and it also affects how much you can rent it without triggering investment property classification. Under IRS rules, a property qualifies as a second home (not a rental property) if you use it personally for more than 14 days per year or more than 10% of the days it's rented, whichever is greater.

Cross that line and the tax treatment changes. So does the mortgage story if you bought it as a second home and start treating it as a rental after the fact. Lenders include occupancy agreements in second home mortgages that require you to actually use the property. Misrepresenting intended use at the time of application is mortgage fraud, not a gray area.

If your honest intent is to rent the property out most of the year, apply for an investment property loan from the start. The rate and down payment will be higher, but you won't be misrepresenting your plans.

## Credit Score Expectations

For a conventional second home loan, most lenders want a minimum score of 680, and you'll get better pricing at 740 or above. Scores below 680 make second home financing difficult to find and expensive if you do find it. This isn't a hard floor set by any single rule; lenders apply their own overlays on top of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines. Some are stricter.

A common misconception is that if you qualified easily for your primary mortgage, you'll sail through the second home approval. What changes: the lender is now looking at your ability to absorb two full mortgage payments, your existing home equity position, and whether your savings hold up after a larger down payment. It's a different analysis.
